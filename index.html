A Validated 6-DOF Rocket Flight Dynamics Simulator

Design, staged validation, and the bugs I had to chase down

Abhiram Nimmala · solo build · Python / NumPy / Matplotlib · ~2,600 lines · 2026

View the code on GitHub →

I built a six-degree-of-freedom rocket flight simulator from scratch in Python, one layer at a time, from a bare numerical integrator up to a working flight controller. I gave myself one rule for the whole project: I would not build a layer until I had checked the one underneath it against an answer I already knew. This page covers how it is put together, what the validation actually showed, and the eight bugs I had to diagnose along the way, which turned out to be the part I learned the most from.

1. Why I built it in layers

A flight simulator stacks a lot of physics on top of itself. Integration holds up atmosphere, which holds up propulsion, which holds up rotation, aerodynamics, and finally control. The thing that makes these projects go wrong is subtle: a bug in a lower layer usually does not crash anything. It just makes the rocket fly a little bit off, and a little bit off looks exactly like real aerodynamics when you glance at a plot. You can stare at a wrong simulator for a long time and never notice.

So I built the whole thing to make that impossible. Every one of the six steps comes with its own tests, and each test checks a real physical quantity against a value I can compute independently. Nothing moves up a layer until the layer below it passes. When something does break three steps later, I know the break lives in the step I just touched, because everything under it is already proven.

2. How it is built

LayerWhat it addsChecked against
1RK4 integrator, 3-DOF point massClosed-form projectile
2US Standard Atmosphere 1976, quadratic drag, event detectionPublished density tables; analytic terminal velocity
3Solid motor, mass depletion, launch railTotal-impulse conservation
46-DOF: quaternion attitude, rigid-body rotation, aero torque3-DOF apogee regression; angular-momentum conservation
5Barrowman aerodynamics, Mach-dependent drag, moving CPStatic-margin envelope; compressibility limits
6Closed-loop control: PID + LQR, actuator limitsStep response, disturbance rejection

One decision shaped everything else. I store the state as a flat NumPy array and keep the integrator completely generic, so RK4 has no idea whether it is pushing a 6-element point mass or a 13-element rigid body forward in time. It just sees a vector and a function that returns its derivative. When I went from 3-DOF to full 6-DOF with a quaternion attitude, I did not touch the integrator at all. I also used quaternions instead of Euler angles so there is no gimbal-lock singularity to trip over, and I advance time with an integer step count rather than adding up t += dt, which sounds pedantic until it costs you an afternoon (section 4, bug 1).

3. What the validation showed

Every number here comes out of a test that compares the simulator to something whose answer I already knew.

3.1 The integrator (Step 1)

RK4 lands on the closed-form projectile trajectory to about 5×10⁻¹² m. When I halve the timestep over and over, the error drops by factors of 13.5, 14.9, 15.5, and 15.7, climbing toward the 16 you expect from a fourth-order method. That ratio is the tell that the integrator is actually correct and settling into its asymptotic regime, not just producing numbers that look fine.

3.2 Atmosphere and drag (Step 2)

I implemented the piecewise US Standard Atmosphere 1976 out to 86 km, and it matches published density tables to within 0.043%. Terminal velocity is more interesting, because I never coded it anywhere. It falls out of the drag model on its own, and under constant density it agrees with the closed form to 3×10⁻¹¹ m/s.

3.3 Propulsion and powered flight (Step 3)

I wrote a parser for RASP .eng thrust curves, the format thrustcurve.org publishes and OpenRocket reads, and used it to drive mass depletion proportional to impulse under a launch-rail constraint. Apogee is caught with a bisection event detector, so the trajectory stops exactly at the top instead of sailing past it by a fraction of a step.

3.4 Six-degree-of-freedom dynamics (Step 4)

The full state has 13 elements: position, velocity, an attitude quaternion, and body-frame angular velocity. I checked it three ways. At zero angle of attack the 6-DOF model reproduces the 3-DOF apogee with no difference at all, which tells me adding rotation did not quietly break translation. The quaternion norm holds to 2×10⁻¹⁶ across 30,000 steps. And torque-free rotation conserves angular momentum and energy to about 10⁻¹⁴. My gimbal-lock metric reads 2.0 at zero pitch and 3,141,593 at ninety degrees, so the singularity is something I can watch happen in the numbers rather than something I just claim is there.

3.5 Aerodynamics (Step 5)

Barrowman normal-force coefficients come from the nose cone, body tube, and fin geometry, with a Prandtl–Glauert compressibility correction and a transonic drag rise that peaks around 2.5× the subsonic value near Mach 1.1. As the motor burns, the reference rocket's static margin walks from 1.44 to 2.85 calibers. That sits inside the usual 1-to-2 caliber rule of thumb and is least stable at liftoff, which is how a real rocket behaves.

3.6 Closed-loop control (Step 6)

I made the pitch-plane plant deliberately unstable open-loop, gave it a gimballed motor, and put two controllers against it: a PID I tuned by hand, and an LQR whose gain I found by solving the Riccati equation with backward integration instead of reaching for SciPy. The LQR settles a 10° step in 1.13 s with no overshoot. The PID takes 3.37 s and overshoots by 30%. Hit them both with a wind gust and the LQR holds the peak deviation to 0.55° while the PID lets it reach 2.27°. The actuator is not idealized either: it has a deflection limit, a rate limit, and first-order lag.

4. The bugs I had to chase down

This is the part I actually want to talk about. The validation harness earned its keep by catching eight problems that all looked like physics and none of them were. Each one taught me a different way a simulation can lie to you.

1. Float accumulation that looked like integrator error. Running time forward with t += dt means the final time lands somewhere slightly different for every timestep, so my convergence study was comparing solutions at times that did not match, and that mismatch buried the real error. The smallest timestep came out looking the worst, which is backwards. Switching to an integer step count fixed it.
2. A terminal-velocity "failure" that was correct. I dropped a sphere 2 km and it hit at 127 m/s against a closed form of 131.7. I went looking for a drag bug and there wasn't one. Air thins as you climb, so terminal velocity starts higher up top and falls on the way down, and 2 km simply is not far enough for it to converge. I split the test in two: a constant-density case that isolates the drag law, and a real-atmosphere case that shows the non-convergence on purpose.
3. I tested the wrong Euler pair for gimbal lock. My first demo used (yaw +d, roll −d) against (yaw −d, roll +d), which does not collapse. With ZYX ordering, a 90° pitch maps body-x onto −z, so it is yaw minus roll that degenerates. Which convention you pick decides which motion breaks, and that is exactly why I keep quaternions underneath.
4. A one-sided assertion that hid a bad rocket. My stability test only checked margin > 1.0, so a 5.9-caliber rocket sailed through without complaint. A 5.9-caliber rocket is wildly overstable and would not fly. The moment I made the check two-sided it caught it.
5. A modeling artifact dressed up as a stability result. Capping the Prandtl–Glauert correction at Mach 0.8 handed the fins a 1.67× effectiveness boost, which then reported a comfortable 3.6-caliber margin at burnout. None of it was real. I cap it at 0.7 now, and the code says outright that past that point the value is held constant, not predicted.
6. A drag curve with a step in it. The transonic rise ended at 2.06× and the supersonic decay started at 2.50×, which left a visible jump at Mach 1.2. No assertion caught this one; I caught it by looking at the plot. A force model with a discontinuity in it makes the integrator thrash.
7. A sign error that made the controller push the wrong way. I defined error as target − theta, but the plant needs positive gimbal to fix positive pitch, so the loop was positive feedback. The vehicle left faster with the controller on than with it off.
8. A stiff ODE pretending to be a control failure. I modeled the "ideal" actuator with a 1µs time constant, which gives an eigenvalue of −10⁶ against a 1 ms step. RK4 cannot integrate that, so the ideal case appeared to blow up at every gain I tried. It read like a control result and it was pure numerical instability. I fixed it by setting the deflection directly instead of integrating toward it.

5. Where it falls short

I would rather name the limits than pretend they are not there. A simulator you cannot poke holes in is one you do not really understand.

  • Barrowman assumes a small angle of attack (under about 10°), subsonic flow, a slender body, and thin fins.
  • The Prandtl–Glauert correction is held constant above Mach 0.7 rather than extrapolated.
  • The transonic drag model is a shape fit, not a first-principles calculation.
  • Control design is pitch-plane only. I do not model roll coupling.
  • There is no wind in the trajectory integrator. Wind shows up only as a disturbance in the control step.
  • Everything is validated against closed-form solutions and published tables. I have not checked it against OpenRocket or against real flight data yet.

6. What I want to do next

Two things. The first is an outside check: build the reference vehicle in OpenRocket with a certified .eng motor from thrustcurve.org, fly it, and compare apogee, max-Q, and drag against my simulator. The comparison harness is already written with empty slots waiting for the numbers. The second is a question the simulator is built to answer. Under realistic wind and thrust-misalignment dispersion, which of these fidelity features actually move the predicted landing point, and by how much? That is a Monte Carlo ablation: switch each modeling layer off in turn and measure how much of the output variance it was responsible for. That is the version of this project that turns into a real finding instead of a description of what I built.
